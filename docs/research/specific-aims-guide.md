# Guide to Writing Specific Aims

This guide teaches you how to develop and write a research proposal in the NIH Specific Aims format. This is a standard, learnable format used in professional and educational scientific research contexts.

## What are Specific Aims?

Specific Aims is a **one-page document** that clearly articulates:
- What scientific problem you're investigating
- Why it matters
- What you will do to test your hypothesis
- What you expect to discover or learn

It's the "executive summary" of a research proposal.

## Structure

A typical Specific Aims page has these sections:

### 1. Overall Objective (2-3 sentences)

State your big-picture goal in language an educated non-specialist can understand.

**Educational Example:** "The overall objective is to develop machine learning approaches for automatically detecting structures in microscopy images, enabling efficient analysis of tissue samples."

### 2. Research Problem & Significance (1/2 page)

Explain the problem you're addressing and why it matters.

**Structure:**
- What is currently known? (background)
- What is NOT known or not yet solved? (the gap)
- Why does this matter? (significance)
- Who would benefit? (impact)

**Educational Example:** 
"Tissue analysis currently requires manual counting and classification, which is labor-intensive and prone to human error. While computer vision has been applied successfully to other medical imaging tasks, specific tissue types have unique properties that require specialized approaches. Developing automated analysis would improve research efficiency and allow systematic study of tissue organization patterns."

### 3. Central Hypothesis (1-2 sentences)

State what you believe to be true and will test. This is your educated guess based on prior work and reasoning.

**Educational Example:** "We hypothesize that image processing techniques combined with machine learning can automatically classify tissue structures with accuracy comparable to trained human analysts."

### 4. Specific Aim 1 (3-4 sentences each)

Write 2-3 specific aims. Each aim should:
- Start with an action verb ("To determine...", "To develop...", "To investigate...", "To compare...")
- State exactly what you'll do
- Explain your approach
- Describe measurable success criteria

**Educational Example Aim 1:** "To develop and test a machine learning model for classifying structures in microscopy images. We will prepare annotated training data, experiment with different computational approaches, and measure performance on independent test images. Success is defined as achieving classification accuracy above baseline methods."

**Educational Example Aim 2:** "To validate that our new implementation reproduces results from established reference methods. We will compare outputs from our approach against reference implementations using quantitative metrics and qualitative assessment. Implementation is considered valid if results align with reference methods within expected variation."

**Educational Example Aim 3:** "To explore which computational approaches are most promising for future investigation. We will compare different strategies in terms of accuracy, computational cost, and resource requirements. This exploration will inform which approaches merit deeper study."

### 5. Feasibility & Timeline (1/4 page)

Briefly explain how this is achievable and on what timeline.

**Educational Example:** "This project is feasible because training data is available, computational resources are sufficient (Prime8 with 1TB RAM and 100+ CPUs), and your mentors have expertise in both machine learning and tissue analysis. Timeline: Weeks 1-3 for foundational skills, Weeks 5-6 for literature review and reproduction of reference methods, Weeks 7-10 for your new implementation, Weeks 11-12 for validation and next steps."

## Tips for Writing Effective Specific Aims

**Do:**
- Use clear, direct language (avoid jargon; define necessary technical terms)
- Be specific about what you'll measure ("reduce classification error by 10%" vs. "improve performance")
- Focus on what you'll discover or learn, not just the methods
- Make it testable (could someone check if you succeeded?)
- Ground it in available resources, data, and timeframe

**Don't:**
- Make unsupported claims ("prove with 100% certainty", "this will revolutionize the field")
- Write complicated sentences
- Propose experiments you cannot actually perform with available resources
- Focus only on methods rather than scientific learning outcomes
- Make it longer than one page
- Invent preliminary results or preliminary data you don't have

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | How to Fix |
|---------|-----------|
| Too vague ("improve the system") | Be specific: "achieve 90% classification accuracy using data augmentation" |
| Impossible scope | Narrow your scope to what you can actually do in the timeframe |
| Only methods, no questions | Rephrase: "What does this method reveal?" instead of "We will write code" |
| No feasibility discussion | Explain briefly: What data do you have? What tools? What's the timeline? |
| Unsupported claims | Remove claims like "novel breakthrough" or "unprecedented results" unless grounded in evidence |

## Educational vs. Professional Context

As a high school intern, your Specific Aims is **educational** rather than professional. This means:

**Emphasis:** Learning by doing, understanding methods, contributing to lab knowledge

**Realistic scope:** Achievable within 12 weeks with mentorship

**Honest assessment:** Acknowledge challenges, limitations, and areas for future work

**Collaboration:** Your work builds on established methods and mentors' expertise

**Professional Specific Aims** (for grant applications) would claim novelty and impact. Your version should focus on solid scientific thinking and learning.

## The One-Page Limit

Everything must fit on **ONE page** of text. Tips:
- 11-12 point font
- Single spacing or 1.15 spacing
- Narrow margins (0.5-1 inch)
- Remove decorative graphics
- Use the space for clarity

**Ruthless editing:** If it doesn't directly support your central hypothesis, remove it.

## Developing Your Proposal (Weeks 3-12)

### Week 3-4: Identify a Research Question

- What biological or computational problem interests you?
- What would you want to know more about?
- What connects to work you're learning in Weeks 1-3?

### Week 5-6: Literature Review

- What's already known about this topic?
- What gaps or unanswered questions exist?
- What challenges have others encountered?

### Week 7-8: Draft Your Aims

- Write your first draft without worrying about perfection
- Be specific about what you'll do and measure
- Explain why this matters

### Week 9-10: Refine with Mentor Feedback

- Eisa and Don will help identify unclear parts
- Revise for clarity, feasibility, and scientific thinking
- Iterate until the proposal is solid

### Week 11-12: Polish and Present

- Create final version with correct formatting
- Practice explaining your work to others
- Present to mentors and discuss next steps

## Common Questions

**Q: Can my research question change?**
A: Yes! As you learn more, your question might evolve. That's normal research. Just update your Specific Aims as your understanding deepens.

**Q: What if my first attempt isn't that good?**
A: Perfect. Writing is iterative. Mentors expect your first draft to need work. That's what revision is for.

**Q: Can I build on SATINN research for my proposal?**
A: Yes, if it interests you. Or choose something completely different. Your mentors want you excited about your research.

**Q: What happens if I can't complete all my aims in 12 weeks?**
A: That's okay. Science is unpredictable. Document what you learned, what worked, what didn't, and what you'd do next time. That's valuable too.
